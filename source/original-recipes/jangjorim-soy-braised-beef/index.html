Jangjorim Soy-Braised Beef

Korean · Medium · Makes one jar (a month of tables)

Jump to Recipe Method Troubleshooting Storage
**Stream: heritage — the jar that owns the month.** Jangjorim is the banchan that behaves like a preserve: a lean cut of beef braised in soy until the fibers pull apart into strands, packed into a jar with its braising liquid, whole garlic cloves gone amber, and bird's eye chilies — and then it serves the table for weeks, a spoonful of shreds over rice at every meal until the jar runs dry. It is salty-sweet, intensely savory, the "rice thief" category's champion, and at the grill table it is the bowl that outlasts every other banchan on the board. One afternoon of mostly-untended braising buys a month of meals.

The braise

- **The two-stage liquid is the architecture.** Stage one poaches the beef in plain aromatized water (the fibres relax, the beef's own flavor builds the broth); stage two reduces a soy-sweet braise with that broth as its base. Skipping stage one and boiling the beef in soy from the start makes over-salty, tight-fibered shreds — the common shortcut and the common disappointment. - Count the garlic: the ten whole cloves of stage two are not a typo. They braise to spreading-soft, amber-sweet, and the jar's second treasure — regulars fish for them first.

Doneness & Quality Checkpoints

  • The poach: barest simmer, 40 minutes, skimmed — never a rolling boil.
  • The shred: hand-torn along the fibers, 5 cm lengths, never knife-diced.
  • The braise: reduced to wet-not-soupy, shreds glossy mahogany, garlic amber and soft.
  • The jar: packed hot, cooled uncovered, clean dry spoon forever.

Finish & Plate

A small bowl of shreds with a spoonful of the liquid, one whole garlic and one chili fished out per serving — the portions are small by design; jangjorim is eaten by the spoonful over rice, not by the bowl. At the grill table it sits between the fresh banchan and the meat, the month-long jar among the day's bowls.

Jangjorim Soy-Braised Beef

Prep: 15 minutes · Cook: 1 hour 15 minutes · Total: About 1 hour 30 minutes, mostly untended · Makes one jar (a month of tables)

Ingredients

The beef and the first stage

  • 600 g lean beef (eye of round, brisket point's lean half, or shank), one piece, tied if it wants to fall apart
  • Water to cover (about 1.5 liters)
  • 6 cloves garlic (about 24 g), crushed, and a thumb of ginger (about 15 g), sliced — the poaching aromatics
  • 1 tsp salt (about 5 g), in the poach

The braise

  • 150 ml soy sauce
  • 150 ml of the poaching liquid
  • 3 tbsp corn syrup or 2 tbsp honey (about 50 g)
  • 2 tbsp mirin (30 ml)
  • 10 cloves garlic (about 40 g), whole, peeled
  • 4–6 bird's eye chilies (about 8 g), whole, stems on
  • 1 tsp toasted sesame oil (5 ml), off heat
  • 1 tbsp toasted sesame seeds (about 8 g)

Instructions

  1. **The poach (40 minutes).** The beef in cold water with the aromatics, brought to the barest simmer — a bubble every few seconds, 90 °C-plus but never a rolling boil (a hard boil tightens the fibers this recipe is trying to shred) — skimmed once at the come-up, lid ajar, 40 minutes to just-tender. Checkpoint — a chopstick slides into the meat with firm resistance — cooked through, not yet falling. Lift the beef to a board (save the liquid — it is stage two's base), rest it 10 minutes, and slice across the grain into 5 mm strips, then tear the strips by hand into shreds along the fibers. The hand-tearing (not knife-cutting) is what gives jangjorim its signature fibrous pull.
  2. **The braise (25–30 minutes).** The shredded beef, soy, 150 ml of the poaching liquid, syrup, mirin, the ten garlic cloves, and the chilies into the wide pan — at a steady simmer now, lid off, stirring every 5 minutes as the liquid reduces. Checkpoint — at 20 minutes the liquid has dropped below the beef's level and the pan's sounds deepen from bubbling to a slow sizzle; the shreds are turning glossy mahogany and the garlic has gone amber at its edges. The target is a wet-not-soupy mass — spoonable with liquid just filling the gaps, not a dry stir-fry and not a stew.
  3. **The jar and the month.** Off the heat, sesame oil and seeds through, and pack into a clean glass jar while hot — the residual heat and the salt content are the keeping. Cool uncovered, then lid and refrigerate. Checkpoint — the eating test, warm from the pan and again cold from the fridge on day three: the shreds should pull into long savory-sweet strands, the garlic crushes into them like a paste, the chili sits whole for the brave — and over hot rice, one spoonful should make the bowl disappear faster than anything else on the table. That is the rice-thief standard, and this jar meets it.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- The jar keeps 3–4 weeks refrigerated. The braise is the make-ahead, portioned by the week. The spent braising liquid's next career: eggs (#B19's entire recipe). - Variations: the quail-egg batch (#B19); braised shiitake alongside the beef for the two-texture jar; a spoon of jangjorim over a plain fried egg is the single best rice bowl this site's whole banchan section produces.

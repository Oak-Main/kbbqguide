Neobiani Royal-Style Grilled Beef

Korean · Medium · Serves 4

Jump to Recipe Method Troubleshooting Storage
**Stream: heritage — the household-book line.** Neobiani is the old beef sheet: tender loin cut wide and thin, its faces worked with the knife tip until the meat yields, coated in a soy-sesame seasoning set, and grilled fast over charcoal. The method enters print in the late 1800s household compilation *Siuijeonseo* under the heading 너븨안이, is witnessed again in 1917 as a sirloin or tenderloin sheet finished with sesame salt on a charcoal grate and cut into squares after the fire, and is written down once more in 1957 from palace-kitchen memory with sugar and the serve-hot-at-the-meal rule added. That is the lineage this record cooks. It is not "the original bulgogi" — the sweet pear-slurry dish (#M01) is a postwar Seoul development that shares an ancestor but not a kitchen — and the fastest way to ruin neobiani is to import that cousin's habits: no pear here, no gochujang, no overnight soak, and never a grind.

- **The knife is the technique.** Jankaljil — fine scoring with the knife tip, both faces, on the bias in a crosshatch, rows about 5 mm apart. You are cutting surface fibers, not hacking: each stroke is shallow, a few millimeters, and the sheet should relax and widen visibly as you work it. This mechanical tenderizing is what lets a 4–5 mm sheet eat tender at the table without fruit, enzymes, or hours of soak. A sheet that gets ground or minced has left this recipe entirely — that is the yaksanjeok and tteokgalbi road (#M09). - **Build the paste.** Stir the soy, sesame oil, ground sesame salt, garlic, scallion, and pepper into a thick paste. It should be assertive and smell of sesame first, soy second. Sugar only if you are cooking the 1957 layer — and if a guest asks why it's there, the honest answer is that the palace memory added it, the 1800s sheet did not carry it. - **The rest is short.** Coat each sheet lightly on both sides, stack them in a dish, and let them rest 30–60 minutes at cool room temperature — a coat, not a bath. The 1957 witness is explicit about the clock: rest, then grill at the meal, serve hot, do not hold. This is the opposite of the Seoul lane's overnight slurry, and the difference is the point. - **Build a charcoal fire burned to gray-edged coals** — hot but not flaming, a grate surface in the 230 °C range where a drop of water vanishes on contact. Neobiani's paste wants fast dry heat; a flame-up will take the sesame oil's perfume away in smoke.

Doneness & Quality Checkpoints

  • The sheet: 4–5 mm, against the grain, crosshatch-scored both faces, paste-coated 30–60 minutes — never ground, never soaked.
  • The fire: gray-edged charcoal, dry grid marks, mahogany not black, flipped once at the weeping edge.
  • The pull: raw red just gone, faintly rosy center, fingertip spring.
  • The finish: squares cut after the fire, sesame salt over, served hot with the pan-truth named.

Finish & Plate

The squares go to the table's center while they still carry the grate's heat, on a warm platter with the sesame salt alongside. Rice, lettuce and perilla, the gireumjang (#SA02) bowl, and the honest quiet of a dish that needs no lacquer — the last sheet off the grate goes to whoever has been running the fire, by the table's oldest rule.

Neobiani Royal-Style Grilled Beef

Prep: 25 minutes, plus 30–60 minutes resting · Cook: 10 minutes · Total: About 1 hour 15 minutes, mostly resting · Serves 4

Ingredients

The beef

  • 700 g beef tenderloin or sirloin, cut against the grain into wide sheets, 4–5 mm thick (about 8 sheets, palm-sized)

The seasoning set (the soy-sesame paste)

  • 3 tbsp good soy sauce or soup soy (45 ml) — the closest modern shelf equivalent to the old jinjang
  • 1.5 tbsp toasted sesame oil (about 22 ml)
  • 2 tbsp toasted sesame seeds, half ground to a coarse salt (about 16 g total — this is kkaesogeum, sesame salt)
  • 4 cloves garlic (about 16 g), very finely minced
  • 2 scallions (about 30 g), finely sliced
  • 1/2 tsp black pepper (about 2 g)
  • Optional, and dated if used: 1 tsp sugar (about 4 g) — sugar enters the written court line in the 1957 write-down, not earlier; include it as the mid-century layer and say so at the table

The fire and finish

  • Oak or lump charcoal (about 1 kg), or a ripping cast-iron pan as the honest indoor bridge
  • Sesame salt and a few whole toasted sesame seeds, for the finish

The table

  • Steamed rice (about 600 g cooked), lettuce and perilla ssam, gireumjang (#SA02) — the sesame-oil salt dip is the period-honest companion; pajeori (#F02) and kimchi (#B01) beside

Instructions

  1. **Grill the sheets one at a time, or two if the grate is wide.** Lay the sheet flat, single layer, and let it sit undisturbed 60–90 seconds — the soy film sets and takes color, and the smell should be sesame and browning beef, not burning sugar. The grill boss's clock here is the edge: when the rim of the sheet pales and beads a little juice, it is time. Checkpoint — the underside, peeked at with tongs, is browned in a dry grid from the grate with no black — a sheet past mahogany into black has crossed from the sesame-soy register into bitterness, and the coals are too eager. Raise the grate a hand-width.
  2. **Flip once, finish fast.** The second side takes 45–60 seconds. Pull the sheet when the raw red is just gone; the scoring has already guaranteed tenderness, so the only variable left is juice, and juice leaves with every extra second. Straight to the board. Checkpoint — press the sheet's center with a fingertip: it should give and spring, not resist. A test piece eaten standing at the grill should taste sesame, soy, smoke, beef — in that order of arrival, with sweetness absent unless the 1957 sugar layer is in, in which case it arrives last and faint.
  3. **Cut after the fire, in squares.** The 1917 witness is specific and it is good practice: the whole sheet comes off the grate, rests half a minute, then is cut into neat squares — two or three cuts each way — for the platter. The square is the old table's portion logic, and it gives every diner an edge piece and a center piece. Sesame salt and a few whole seeds over the cut faces. Checkpoint — the squares on the platter should show the crosshatch scoring crisped at the intersections, browned faces, and an interior still faintly rosy at 4–5 mm. Squares that bleed red went too short; squares gray through went too long — and gray-through is the more common failure, because cooks trained on thin bulgogi shavings underestimate how fast a scored sheet's center arrives.
  4. **Serve hot, in the moment, with the pan-truth stated.** This dish dies holding: the 1957 line is "grill at the meal," and every minute on a warm platter trades sesame perfume for steam. The ssam build for this record is the quiet one: leaf, a bare finger-swipe of gireumjang (#SA02), one or two squares while they are still hot, fold, one bite. Heavy ssamjang buries the sheet's register; save it for the pork records (#M10). Checkpoint — the eating test at the table's first ssam: the wide, scored-but-intact bite — tender to the tooth with a structure thin bulgogi never has, smoke in the crosshatch, sesame on the finish. If the first taste is sweetness, the wrong cousin is on the plate.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- Neobiani does not store as itself: the sheet's perfume is minutes old at service. Extra sheets hold pasted, refrigerated, one day; extra cooked squares become the finest kimchi fried rice in this category by a distance. - Variations: the 1957 layer (sugar in, serve-hot doctrine stated); the Gwangyang clock (thinner sheet, toss at the table, grill instantly); the yaksanjeok skewer road and the tteokgalbi grind (#M09) are different recipes, linked by history, separated by the knife.

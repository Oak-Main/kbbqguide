Jjukkumi Gui Grilled Baby Octopus

Korean · Medium · Serves 3–4

Jump to Recipe Method Troubleshooting Storage
**Stream: restaurant — the tent's signature.** Jjukkumi are baby octopus the length of a hand, and their grill record is the drinking table's favorite version of the octopus idea: whole little octopus tossed in a sweet-spicy gochujang marinade, seared fast on a hot pan or tabletop dome until the sauce caramelizes into glaze, and eaten entire — head, tentacles, everything — with rice or straight off the pan with soju. Where the nakji record (#SF05) pre-simmers a bigger octopus, jjukkumi are small enough to grill from raw, which is the entire reason they own the lane: no simmer, no pounding, just the marinade clock and a hot pan. Who cooks what: the pan belongs to whoever loses the first round of drinks-buying. Tongs rotate with the batches.

- **Check the cleaning.** Even "cleaned" frozen jjukkumi often keep their beaks (the hard mouth, at the center of the tentacles' underside) and sometimes the ink sac or innards in the head. Squeeze the beak out with your fingertips over the sink; turn back the head and pull whatever is inside. Thirty seconds per octopus, non-negotiable — a beak in a glaze-bright bite ends the meal's mood. - **Marinate 30 minutes, refrigerated.** Toss the dried octopus with the whisked marinade in a bowl; the small bodies take flavor fast, and past an hour the salt begins curing them tight. - **The pan-mates' job:** onion and scallion go under and around the octopus on the pan — they burn sweet in the glaze's runoff and become half the platter's eating. Cut them ahead; the cooking has no assembly time. - Fire plan: a tabletop dome or a wide pan at a solid medium-high (oil at the shimmer, about 200 °C) — hot enough to sear in the marinade's water, not so hot the sugars scorch before it evaporates.

Doneness & Quality Checkpoints

  • The octopus: beaked and cleaned, dried, marinated 30 minutes, whole.
  • The pan: hot enough to sizzle hard on landing; onion bed down first; glaze reduces to lacquer in 4–6 minutes.
  • The pull: firm-plump glossy bodies, opaque at a test cut, snap-tender at the tooth.
  • The table: off the sizzling pan, rice and cold crunch in reach, platter empty before the pan cools.

Finish & Plate

The pan itself is the platter — trivet, sesame, tongs, and the table gathers. Rice bowls for the steady hands, lettuce or perilla for the hottest pieces, and the mu sangchae bowl passing hand to hand. The last octopus goes to the pan's cook; the tent's law is older than the recipe.

Jjukkumi Gui Grilled Baby Octopus

Prep: 20 minutes, plus 30 minutes marinating · Cook: 10 minutes at the table · Total: About 1 hour 10 minutes, mostly resting · Serves 3–4

Ingredients

The octopus

  • 800 g baby octopus (jjukkumi), cleaned — beaks and innards removed, bodies left whole; thawed fully and patted dry if frozen

The marinade

  • 3 tbsp gochujang (about 60 g)
  • 2 tbsp honey or corn syrup (about 40 g)
  • 2 tbsp soy sauce (30 ml)
  • 1 tbsp mirin (15 ml)
  • 1 tbsp toasted sesame oil (15 ml)
  • 4 cloves garlic (about 16 g), grated
  • 1 thumb ginger (about 10 g), grated
  • 2 tsp gochugaru (about 6 g) — the heat dial for the drinking table
  • 1 tbsp toasted sesame seeds (about 8 g)

The pan-mates

  • 1 onion (about 150 g), thick-sliced into arcs
  • 2 scallions (about 30 g), cut into 5 cm lengths
  • 100 g cabbage or perilla leaves, rough-torn (optional but traditional)

The table

  • Steamed rice (about 500 g cooked), mu sangchae (about 100 g) or cucumber banchan (#B07), soju's ceremony

Instructions

  1. **Onion down first, octopus over.** Lay the onion arcs across the hot oiled pan as a bed, and spread the marinated jjukkumi over and around them in a single layer — the bodies will curl and the tentacles tighten into little crowns as they hit. Checkpoint — a hard sizzle and steam rising off the marinade's water, sweet chile in the air within a minute. If the pan is quiet and pooling liquid, it was not hot enough — pull the batch, reheat, restart; jjukkumi boiled in marinade is rubber in glaze.
  2. **Turn with the tongs, two minutes at a time, glaze as it reduces.** The marinade's water cooks off and what remains on the pan thickens to a glaze that lacquers each octopus as you turn them through it — four to six minutes total. Scallions in for the last two. The bodies are done when they are firm-plump, glossy red-brown, and a body cut at the pan reads opaque to its center. Checkpoint — the pan should be nearly dry, everything coated brick-glossy, onion edges char-sweet and translucent. One test octopus whole: snap-tender (they are small — this is the tender side of octopus's law), sweet-heat lacquer, the ocean's sweetness under it. If any bite is rubbery, that piece needed one more minute in the reducing glaze — small octopus tenderizes briefly under heat before it tightens, unlike squid's instant law.
  3. **Pan to the table on its trivet, sesame over, straight off the pan.** Jjukkumi's window is while the glaze still sizzles; rice in one hand, tongs in the other, the cabbage or perilla to wrap the sweetest, hottest ones. Checkpoint — the table test: a whole octopus over rice in one bite-set — glaze edge, snap, chew, heat blooming late — with the cold radish between rounds. The platter should be empty before the pan stops sizzling; that is not a metaphor, it is the recipe's honest clock.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- Marinated jjukkumi hold 24 hours refrigerated. Cooked, 2 days — chopped into fried rice or bibim-guksu (cold spicy noodles), where the leftover glaze seasons the whole bowl. - Variations: the big-octopus fork with the pre-simmer (#SF05); squid takes this same marinade and the #SF04 clock; a handful of rice cakes tossed onto the pan in the last three minutes is the tent's carb course and entirely correct.

Doraji Muchim Spicy Bellflower Root

Korean · Medium · Serves 4 as banchan

Jump to Recipe Method Troubleshooting Storage
**Stream: heritage — the medicinally minded banchan.** Doraji — bellflower root, the white twisted ribbons of the doraji plant — is the banchan that tastes like the mountain apothecary: faintly bitter, deeply vegetal, crunchier than any root has a right to be after cooking. Dressed with gochugaru, garlic, and sesame, it becomes the traditional partner for oily fish (#SF07's table) and the bowl Korean grandmothers cite for throat health while everyone eats seconds. The technique is salt-cure and squeeze — the bitter compound leaves with the water.

Quantities live in the recipe card.

The bellflower root

The seasoning

- **Split and salt the fresh roots.** Fresh doraji needs a violent rub with coarse salt (the classic treatment: salt between the palms, scrubbing the ribbons hard for two full minutes — the traditional method bruises the fibers and drives the bitter out), then a 30-minute rest, then a rinse and a hard squeeze by the fistful. The water that leaves carries most of the bitterness; the squeeze is the recipe. - Dried doraji skips the rub (it was treated in processing) but needs its long soak and a shorter squeeze.

Doraji Muchim Spicy Bellflower Root

Prep: 25 minutes (plus 30 minutes salting) · Cook: 5 minutes · Total: About 1 hour, mostly waiting · Serves 4 as banchan

Ingredients

The bellflower root

  • 300 g fresh doraji (bellflower root), or 150 g dried, soaked 3 hours
  • 1 tbsp coarse salt (about 15 g), for the 30-minute cure

The seasoning

  • 1.5 tbsp gochugaru (about 9 g)
  • 1 tsp fish sauce or soy sauce (about 5 ml)
  • 2 cloves garlic (about 8 g), grated
  • 1/2 tsp sugar or honey (about 3 g)
  • 1 tsp toasted sesame oil (5 ml)
  • 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds (about 4 g)
  • 1 scallion (about 15 g), sliced
  • A few drops of rice vinegar (about 2 ml), optional

Instructions

  1. Blanch the cured, squeezed ribbons 3–4 minutes in unsalted boiling water — they should turn from opaque white to translucent-edged, still crunchy. Checkpoint — a test ribbon bends into a U without breaking and keeps an audible crunch at the tooth. Overcooked doraji loses the crunch that is its whole identity; pull it while it still seems slightly too firm.
  2. Cold rinse, drain, squeeze once more (gently this time — it has given enough), and cut into shorter lengths if it came in ribbons longer than 8 cm. Checkpoint — the ribbons are dry to the touch and springy.
  3. The seasoning bowl: gochugaru, fish sauce, garlic, sugar, sesame oil worked to a paste; the warm-not-hot ribbons tossed through with the full-minute hand massage the namul family demands (#B16's law), scallions and sesame last. Checkpoint — the eating test: crunch first, the mild-bitter vegetal note under it, the dressing's savory-heat arriving second, and the slight bitterness lingering as the finish — that finish is doraji's signature, the reason it has kept its seat at the table beside sweeter vegetables that try harder to please.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- Keeps 2–3 days refrigerated; the dressed ribbons hold crunch better than the blanched ones alone. Dried doraji, unsoaked, keeps a year in the pantry. - Variations: milder (half the gochugaru) as bibimbap's white vegetable; mixed with cucumber matchsticks for a summer crunch bowl; the blanched ribbons simply dressed with sesame oil and salt — the subtle fork the connoisseurs order.

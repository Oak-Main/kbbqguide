**Stream: heritage — the lunchbox jewels.** Quail eggs, hard-boiled and peeled, braised in the jangjorim soy (#B20's braise) until the whites marble tan and the eggs turn savory through to the yolk's edge: these are the lunchbox's jewels and the drinking table's best one-bite. They run the same braise as the beef record with a shorter clock and one technique of their own — peeling quail eggs without losing half the white.

Quantities live in the recipe card.

The eggs

The braise (a half-batch of #B20's)

- **Boil 4 minutes, shock, peel wet.** Quail eggs boil fast — 4 minutes from the drop for just-set yolks — and the peel works only under water: the shell's membrane releases in the cold shock, and peeling under a thin stream or in a bowl of water saves the whites. Older eggs (a week in the fridge) peel more kindly than fresh; the opposite of the fry-up's law, worth knowing once. - Bring the braise liquids to a simmer while you peel — the eggs should go into hot braise, never cold, to start the marbling at once.

Mechurial Jangjorim Soy-Braised Quail Eggs

Prep: 10 minutes · Cook: 25 minutes · Total: About 35 minutes · Serves 4–6 as banchan (makes about 24 eggs)

Ingredients

The eggs

  • 24 quail eggs (about 480 g)
  • Water to cover; 1 tsp salt (about 5 g); a splash of vinegar (about 5 ml) in the boil

The braise (a half-batch of #B20's)

  • 200 ml water
  • 3 tbsp soy sauce (45 ml)
  • 1 tbsp corn syrup or honey (about 20 g)
  • 2 cloves garlic (about 8 g), crushed
  • 1 tsp mirin (15 ml), optional
  • A few slices of jalapeño or a dried chili (optional, the spicy fork)
  • 1/2 tsp toasted sesame oil (about 2 ml), off heat; sesame seeds to finish (about 2 g)

Instructions

  1. The peeled eggs into the simmering braise, 10 minutes at the barest bubble — this first simmer drives the soy in through the warm shell-less whites. Checkpoint — the whites have gone from white to café-au-lait at the surface; the braise smells of soy and sweet garlic.
  2. Off the heat, and the marinating is the recipe: the eggs sit in the braise 1 hour at room temperature, then refrigerate in their liquid overnight. The color deepens to walnut, the soy reaches the yolk's border, the flavor rounds. Checkpoint — the next day's taste test: cut one — a marbled tan white, a bright yolk with a savory halo at its edge. The egg should taste seasoned-through, not dipped.
  3. Serve drained, lightly wet with the braise, sesame over — and the braise itself goes back to work: it is now an egg-fragrant half-batch of jangjorim soy, ready to braise the beef (#B20) or a second round of eggs (top the liquid up with a splash of water and soy). Checkpoint — the eating test at the drinking table or in the lunchbox: one bite, the savory-sweet white snapping against the rich yolk — the bowl empties two eggs at a time per person, and that ratio is the recipe's only scaling law.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- Keeps 4–5 days refrigerated in their liquid (the make-ahead banchan, improving daily). The spent braise gets one more career (#B20, or round two of eggs). - Variations: the jalapeño-spiced fork; chicken eggs run the same braise at a longer soak (2 days) for the marbled deviled-egg lane; the eggs halved over hot rice with a spoon of the liquid is the lunchbox's crown.

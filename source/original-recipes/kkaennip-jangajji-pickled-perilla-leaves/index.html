Kkaennip Jangajji Pickled Perilla Leaves

Korean · Easy · Makes 1 jar (a month's table)

Jump to Recipe Method Troubleshooting Storage
**Stream: heritage — the layered leaf jar.** Perilla — the broad, jagged, anise-mint leaf that the Korean table treats as both vegetable and wrap — pickled leaf by leaf in a soy-sweet brine: kkaennip jangajji is the most distinctive jar in the banchan tradition, nothing else on any table tasting like it. The craft is in the leaf-by-layer build: each leaf painted, stacked, and pressed, the leaves becoming the pickle's own layers. One leaf over hot rice is a meal's reset; one leaf wrapping a piece of grilled pork (#M10) is the tradition's own ssam-within-a-banchan.

- **Dry the leaves completely — the jar's one rule.** Washed perilla carries surface water that dilutes the seasoning layers and shortens the jar's life; a salad spinner and then an hour on towels does it. Any soft or torn leaves are eaten today, not jarred. - Mix the seasoning paste to a paintable consistency — thin enough to brush, thick enough to cling. Too thick (dry paste) starves the layers; too thin runs to the jar's bottom.

Kkaennip Jangajji Pickled Perilla Leaves

Prep: 30 minutes · Cook: 5 minutes · Total: 35 minutes, ready in 1–2 days · Makes 1 jar (a month's table)

Ingredients

The perilla and salt

  • 40–50 fresh perilla leaves (kkaennip, about 60 g), stemmed, washed, spun thoroughly dry

The seasoning paste (painted between layers)

  • 3 tbsp soy sauce (45 ml)
  • 2 tbsp water (30 ml)
  • 1.5 tbsp honey or corn syrup (about 30 g)
  • 1 tbsp rice vinegar (15 ml)
  • 2 cloves garlic (about 8 g), grated
  • 1 tsp gochugaru (about 3 g)
  • 1 tsp sesame seeds (about 4 g)
  • 1/2 tsp toasted sesame oil (about 2 ml)
  • 2 scallions (about 30 g), minced

Instructions

  1. **The paint-and-stack, leaf by leaf.** One leaf on the board, paste painted across its face with a brush or the back of a spoon, the next leaf laid over, painted, the next — the stack becomes the pickle's structure, every layer's seasoning facing the next leaf's face. Ten leaves to a stack, a stack pressed gently, and the stacks stood or laid into the clean jar, pressed down. Checkpoint — the stack holds together when lifted, and cut at the edge, shows its cross-section: leaf, dark line of seasoning, leaf — the jar's architecture, built.
  2. Any remaining paste and a splash of water over the stack in the jar, pressed once more, and the brine that weeps out over the first hours becomes the jar's own liquid. Lid on, refrigerate. Checkpoint — by the next morning, the leaves have darkened to deep olive-bronze and the jar holds a shallow soy-amber liquid — the pickle has set. Taste one leaf at 24 hours.
  3. **The 48-hour readiness.** Day one is good; day two is the jar's true voice — the leaf's anise-mint tamed to an herb depth, the brine's soy-sweet in every fold, the texture silky-supple. Checkpoint — the eating test, one leaf over hot rice: fold, bite — a complex savory-herbal chew that no other pickle on any table resembles. And the ssam use — one leaf wrapping a hot piece of samgyeopsal with a grain of rice — is this site's best single bite between the meat rounds.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- Keeps 3–4 weeks refrigerated (a month with dry-leaf discipline), the flavor deepening for two. The wept brine seasons fried rice when the leaves run out. - Variations: chopped into the paste for a spoonable version (the quick bowl); the garlic jar (#B23) shares its shelf; a minced leaf into the ssamjang (#SA01) transplants the perilla voice into every wrap at the table.
